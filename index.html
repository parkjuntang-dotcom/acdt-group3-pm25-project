Can healthcare spending buffer polluted air?

Our short answer: higher-spending countries live longer on average, but this dataset does not show that healthcare spending reliably buffers PM2.5. The simple negative association becomes smaller and uncertain after we account for national wealth and urbanisation.

Main finding

Group gap: yes.
Buffering: not established.

Healthcare groups differ in average life expectancy. That is not the same as proving that healthcare spending changes the pollution slope.

How to read this

Association is not causation.

This is an ecological comparison of countries, not a treatment effect for an individual person. The data cover 1,776 country-years from 180 countries.

01 / START HERE

The hypothesis has two different parts.

A difference in average life expectancy between two groups does not automatically mean healthcare spending protects countries from PM2.5.

Part 1 · group contrast

Do higher-spending countries live longer?

Yes, in this 2019 snapshot. Countries above the 5.70% of GDP median had a mean life expectancy of 75.05 years, compared with 70.26 years below the median.

Part 2 · buffering

Does spending change the PM2.5 slope?

Not established. The formal healthcare × PM2.5 interaction has p = 0.652. We cannot claim that higher spending reliably weakens the association.

05 / PLAIN-LANGUAGE GUIDE

What the technical words mean here

Association

Two measurements move together. It does not prove that one caused the other.

Control variable

A country characteristic, such as GDP, that we account for before comparing PM2.5.

Interaction

A test of whether the PM2.5 slope changes with healthcare spending.

Fixed effects

A comparison that focuses more on changes within the same country over time.

06 / THE CONCLUSION

A difference is real.
A mechanism is not yet proven.

The simple PM2.5 coefficient is −0.1600, then becomes +0.0157 and uncertain after adjustment. That sign change is a warning about country context, not evidence that pollution is beneficial.

Policy takeaway: do not treat healthcare spending as a substitute for clean-air policy. Improve air quality and healthcare access as separate goals; our data do not show that one reliably cancels the other.
